Vector Potential · Longitudinal Waves · Plasma Antenna

How the Nikolova–Zimmerman
Plasma Tube Detector Works

Why a DC-biased fluorescent (or neon) plasma tube can detect an incident longitudinally polarized vector-potential wave when ordinary metal antennas cannot.

Q The Question

How does a biased plasma tube (as used by Nikolova and Zimmerman) act as a receiving antenna for longitudinally polarized momentum / vector-potential waves?

1 Core Idea in One Sentence

Free electrons in a low-pressure plasma possess high mobility. An incident longitudinal magnetic vector potential \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) couples directly to their kinetic momentum, modulating the electron drift velocity along the tube axis and producing a detectable RF current.

2 Historical & Experimental Context

In 2011 Robert K. Zimmerman (NP4B / VE3RKZ) published in QEX the paper “Transmission and Reception of Longitudinally-Polarized Momentum Waves.” Subsequent work with Natalia Nikolova (McMaster University) and Zimmerman’s 2013 Journal of Applied Physics paper refined the plasma-antenna concept.

📡
The detector is typically a U-shaped fluorescent lamp or neon tube operated with a modest DC bias current (often a few to tens of milliamperes). The tube is oriented so that its long axis lies parallel to the expected direction of the longitudinal vector potential \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\).

When a longitudinal wave is present, an RF signal appears on the plasma tube that is not explained by ordinary transverse electromagnetic (TEM) coupling. A characteristic experimental signature is that reversing the polarity of the DC bias current reverses the phase of the received RF signal.

3 Why Plasma Electrons Sense the Vector Potential

In classical and quantum electrodynamics the mechanical momentum of a charged particle is related to the canonical momentum by the vector potential:

\[ \mathbf{p}_{\text{mech}} = m\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{can}} - q\mathbf{A} \]

Consequently an oscillating longitudinal \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) produces an oscillating contribution to the electron velocity. Inside a plasma the free-electron density is high and the electrons are only weakly collisional at typical bias pressures, so the velocity response is comparatively large.

⚡
In metal conductors the electrons are tightly bound to the lattice; the effective RF velocity induced by \(\mathbf{A}\) is only a few millimetres per second. In a fluorescent-tube plasma the same \(\mathbf{A}\) can produce RF velocity components of order tens of metres per second — orders of magnitude larger.

Orientation Requirement

For a longitudinally polarized wave the vector potential \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) points along the direction of propagation. The plasma column must therefore be aligned with that axis so that the \(\mathbf{A}\)-induced velocity perturbation lies along the tube and modulates the bias current.

Schematic based on Zimmerman’s Fig. 4(b). A longitudinal vector potential \(\mathbf{A}\) arrives from the left. In the upper leg the electron drift is anti-parallel to \(\mathbf{A}\) and receives a momentum increment \(\Delta p\) to the left; in the lower leg the drift is parallel to \(\mathbf{A}\) and receives \(\Delta p\) to the right. The differential momentum appears as an RF current at the tube terminals that can be extracted into a 50 Ω circuit.

4 Detection Mechanism

Velocity Modulation

The time-varying longitudinal \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) adds an oscillating component to the electron velocity. Because a steady bias current is already flowing, this modulation appears as an RF current superimposed on the DC bias.

Phase Reversal Signature

Reversing the polarity of the DC bias reverses the average electron drift direction. Consequently the phase of the received RF signal also reverses — a distinctive experimental marker reported by Zimmerman and Nikolova.

In the language of the Aharonov–Bohm effect and of the Lorentz force expressed in potentials, the plasma tube functions as a differential detector of the longitudinal component of \(\mathbf{A}\). Ordinary metal antennas respond primarily to the transverse electric and magnetic fields; the plasma’s free electrons give it a unique sensitivity to the potential itself.

🔍
Because the wave under consideration is longitudinal, \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}_{L} = 0\). There is therefore no classical magnetic induction to drive currents in a metal loop. The plasma detector does not rely on \(\mathbf{B}\); it responds directly to \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\).

5 Relation to Scalar-Longitudinal Waves

Within the framework of Extended Electrodynamics (EED) the same longitudinal vector potential \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) that appears in the Zimmerman–Nikolova experiments is an integral part of the scalar-longitudinal wave (SLW). The SLW carries both a longitudinal electric field \(\mathbf{E}_{L}\) and a dynamical scalar field \(C\), while the magnetic field remains zero.

\[ \mathbf{E}_{L} = -\nabla\Phi - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}_{L}}{\partial t},\qquad \mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}_{L} = 0 \]

The plasma tube therefore serves as a practical laboratory receiver for the same class of waves that the Hively bifilar coil is designed to detect. The two antennas exploit different physical mechanisms (gradient-driven current versus electron-velocity modulation by \(\mathbf{A}\)), yet both are matched to the irrotational sector of the electromagnetic field.

6 Summary

Key Takeaways

  • A DC-biased plasma tube contains free electrons with high mobility.
  • An incident longitudinal vector potential \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) couples to the electrons via the canonical-momentum relation, modulating their drift velocity.
  • The tube must be oriented parallel to \(\mathbf{A}_{L}\) (i.e., along the propagation direction of the longitudinal wave).
  • The resulting RF current is extracted from the bias circuit; reversing bias polarity reverses the signal phase.
  • Because \(\mathbf{B}=0\) for a pure longitudinal wave, ordinary inductive antennas are insensitive; the plasma detector is not.

Primary references: R. K. Zimmerman, QEX July/August 2011; R. K. Zimmerman, J. Appl. Phys. 114, 044907 (2013); related work with N. Nikolova (McMaster University).
